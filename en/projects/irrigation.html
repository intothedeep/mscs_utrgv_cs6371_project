1 Problem

  • A field dries unevenly. Some cells need water now, and others need it later.
  • There is no map of where water is needed. The robots must sense it.
  • Prof. Lu's foraging swarm lists agriculture among its uses [S-LQ-4].
  • Water delivery is foraging in reverse. It carries from one source to many destinations (inference).

2 Goal

  • Use neither a map nor a central planner.
  • Keep the largest part of the field above a moisture threshold, with the least wasted water.

3 How it works in ARGoS

Table 1. ARGoS setup (source: original How it works in ARGoS)
ItemContent
Robots10 to 30 foot-bots (the stock wheeled robot model of ARGoS). There is one water supply zone.
SensorsThe ground sensor reads dryness as the gray level of the floor. The positioning sensor, the proximity sensor and the range-and-bearing sensor (it gives the distance and direction of neighbor robots and passes short messages) are also used. The range-and-bearing sensor carries the recruit messages.
Behavior rulesA robot looks for a dry cell. It goes to the source, fills up, comes back and pours. It broadcasts "dry here" to call nearby robots.
Loop functionsLoop functions (user code that runs at every simulation step) build a hidden moisture grid. Each cell dries at its own rate. The floor color shows moisture through GetFloorColor and is repainted with SetChanged() [S-LQ-9]. Pouring raises moisture. Water poured on a wet cell counts as waste.

Step 1 of 6

At every step, the loop function lowers the moisture of each cell at that cell's own rate.

Step 2 of 6

An empty robot walks at random, or it follows a recruit message toward a reported dry place.

Step 3 of 6

On a dry cell, the robot records its position. Then it goes to the source and fills one unit of water.

Step 4 of 6

It returns to the recorded place and pours on the driest cell within reach.

Step 5 of 6

On the way back, it broadcasts "(x, y) is dry" with the range-and-bearing signal.

Step 6 of 6

Failure test: at time t, switch off k random robots.

Baselines

  • Random: when a robot has filled up, it pours where it stands.
  • Centralized planner (one planning program that knows the full state and commands every robot): it reads the real moisture grid. It sends each robot to the driest unassigned cell. The route uses A* path search (an algorithm that finds the lowest-cost path on a grid). This is the path planning topic of the course.

Variant: demand-driven delivery

  • Supplies pile up at the source. Requests appear at unknown places and times.
  • Key difference: in delivery, the demand is announced when it appears. In irrigation, the demand is hidden and must be sensed.
  • The robots decide among themselves who takes which request. There is no central dispatcher.
  • A swarm wins when there is no infrastructure (disaster relief), when robots fail, or when the scale is large. Otherwise a central dispatcher wins (inference).

5 What to measure

  • Measure the fraction of cells above the moisture threshold, over time and as an average.
  • Measure the wasted water. It is the amount poured on cells already above the threshold.
  • Measure robustness. After k robots fail, how far does that fraction fall?
  • Delivery variant: measure the mean wait time per request and the number of dropped requests.

6 Research potential and venues

  • Open question: how can a swarm share resources among hidden demands without a map (inference).
  • Beyond a demo: compare the recruit rule with a central planner and add a failure test (inference).

9 Risks

  • There are many parameters (drying rate, tank size). Fix them in week 1.
  • In a small arena, the central baseline can win easily. This is a legitimate finding, not a failure (inference).
