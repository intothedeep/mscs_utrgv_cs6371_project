1 Problem

  • Coverage studies usually assume that robots cooperate.
  • In competitive coverage (teams race to cover the same area), robots compete to cover cells first or last [S-LQ-19].
  • Shats, Amir and Agmon found that pursuing the opponent is the key strategy. Q-learning and deep Q-learning did not close the knowledge gap [S-LQ-19].

2 Goal

  • Two foot-bot teams paint floor cells in their color.
  • The team with more cells at time T wins. Compare strategies.

4 Algorithm steps

  1. Step 1 Random walk
  2. Step 2 Sector sweep
  3. Step 3 Frontier seeking
  4. Step 4 Q-learning (stretch)
  5. Step 5 Matches
Step 1 of 5 (strategy A)

Random walk (the robot changes direction at random as it moves).

Step 2 of 5 (strategy B)

Each robot takes a sector and sweeps it. Use boustrophedon (back-and-forth rows, like plowing a field) or a spiral [S-LQ-5].

Step 3 of 5 (strategy C)

Frontier seeking (go to the border between covered and uncovered cells). Go to the nearest cell not owned by my team.

Step 4 of 5 (strategy D, stretch)

Tabular Q-learning (reinforcement learning with a value table for each state and action) on a coarse grid, trained by self-play. The state is the local 3x3 cell colors (inference).

Step 5 of 5

Play round-robin matches between strategies, 10 seeds for each pair.

5 What to measure

  • The final cell share for each team, and the win-rate matrix between strategies.
  • The share-over-time curve.

6 Research potential and venues

  • Open question: what search pattern wins when an opponent repaints your cells (inference).
  • New beyond a demo: a win-rate matrix that shows non-transitive strategies (a rock-paper-scissors pattern), if one appears (inference).
  • Venues (inference): workshops at the International Conference on Autonomous Agents and Multiagent Systems (AAMAS), the International Conference on Swarm Intelligence (ANTS), and Towards Autonomous Robotic Systems (TAROS).
