1 Problem

  • Foraging with pheromone (a scent signal that ants leave on a path) is fast. Foraging means finding food and carrying it to a nest.
  • But one bad robot can lay a false trail.
  • Prof. Lu's lab studies this attack and its defense [S-LQ-2] [S-LQ-21].

2 Goal

  • Show how much a few detractor robots (robots that lay false trails to fool the swarm) cut the food collected by the swarm.
  • Show how much of that loss a simple defense wins back.

3 How it works in ARGoS

Table 1. ARGoS setup (source: original How it works in ARGoS)
ItemContent
Robots20 to 40 foot-bots (the stock wheeled robot model of ARGoS). A few are detractors. Their light-emitting diode (LED) color is red.
SensorsThe ground sensor reads the floor. The proximity sensor avoids obstacles. The range-and-bearing sensor (it gives the distance and direction of neighbor robots and passes short messages) is optional and shares trail ends. The positioning sensor is also used.
Behavior rulesRobots search by random walk. On finding food, a robot returns to the nest and leaves pheromone. When it senses pheromone, it follows the strongest side.
Loop functionsLoop functions (user code that runs at every simulation step) build the virtual pheromone on a floor grid. GetFloorColor paints the grid color. SetChanged() repaints it at every step. The grid evaporates over time [S-LQ-9].
FoodFood is a set of floor points. It works like the foraging demo of argos3-examples [S-LQ-9].

4 Algorithm steps

  1. Step 1 Decay
  2. Step 2 Honest robots
  3. Step 3 Detractors
  4. Step 4 Following a trail
  5. Step 5 Defense
Step 1 of 5

At every step, the loop function lowers all pheromone cells by a fixed ratio.

Step 2 of 5

An honest robot returns carrying food. It adds pheromone along the path from the food to the nest.

Step 3 of 5

A detractor adds pheromone along a path to an empty area.

Step 4 of 5

A searching robot reads the pheromone in the cell under it. It follows the gradient (the direction of steepest increase).

Step 5 of 5 (defense)

If a robot follows a trail and finds no food, it marks the trail end as "suspect". Suspect ends are grouped by distance and ignored.

5 What to measure

  • Measure food collected per 10 minutes of simulation, against the number of detractors (0, 1, 2, 4).
  • Draw the same curve with and without the defense. Run each with 10 seeds (starting values of the random number generator).
  • Measure the time spent on false trails in robot-seconds.

6 Research potential and venues

  • Open question: how robust is stigmergy (indirect talk through marks left in the environment) against hostile robots [S-LQ-2].
  • Beyond a demo: a cheap defense that needs no communication, or a scaling curve of detractor share against loss.
  • Prof. Lu's defense applies density-based spatial clustering of applications with noise (DBSCAN, a method that groups dense points and treats sparse points as noise) to pheromone trails. It also adds swarm grouping and adaptive timing [S-LQ-21].
  • The contribution would be a simpler new rule compared against the reimplementation (inference).
  • Venue candidates (inference): Towards Autonomous Robotic Systems (TAROS, an autonomous robotics conference where Prof. Lu published this line of work [S-LQ-2]), International Conference on Swarm Intelligence (ANTS, a swarm intelligence conference), International Conference on Ubiquitous Robots (UR, a robotics conference where Prof. Lu published in 2024 [S-LQ-3]).

9 Risks

  • With a fine grid, repainting the floor at every step can slow the simulation. Use a coarse grid (inference).
  • It overlaps with the defense Prof. Lu already published. Present it as one reproduction plus one new rule.
