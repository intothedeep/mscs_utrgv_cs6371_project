## Project: Pheromone foraging under misleading trails

Rank 1. Score R3 F3 Fit3 = 27.
Course topics: swarm robotics, sensors.

### Problem
- Pheromone foraging is fast, but one bad robot can lay a false trail.
- Qi Lu's lab studies this attack and a defense [S-LQ-2], [S-LQ-21].

### Goal
- Show how much a few "detractor" robots cut swarm foraging, and how much a simple defense recovers.

### How it works in ARGoS
- Robots: 20 to 40 foot-bots, a few marked as detractors (LED color red).
- Sensors: ground sensor (reads floor), proximity (avoid), range-and-bearing (optional, share trail ends), positioning.
- Behaviour rules: random walk to search. On food, return to nest and drop pheromone. Follow strongest pheromone when sensed.
- Loop functions: virtual pheromone as a floor grid. `GetFloorColor` paints the grid shade, `SetChanged()` repaints it each step, the grid evaporates over time [S-LQ-9].
- Food items as floor spots, the same way the argos3-examples foraging demo does it [S-LQ-9].

### Algorithm sketch
1. Each step, loop functions decay every pheromone cell by a fixed rate.
2. A returning honest robot adds pheromone along its path from food to nest.
3. A detractor adds pheromone along a path to an empty area.
4. A searching robot senses the cell under it and follows the gradient.
5. Defense: a robot that follows a trail and finds no food marks that trail end as "suspect". Suspect ends are clustered by distance and ignored.

### What to measure
- Food collected per 10 minutes (simulated) versus number of detractors (0, 1, 2, 4).
- Same curve with and without defense. 10 seeds each.
- Time wasted on false trails (robot-seconds).

### Research potential and venues
- Open question: how robust is stigmergy (indirect communication through the environment) to adversarial agents [S-LQ-2].
- New beyond a demo: a cheap defense that uses no communication, or a scaling curve (detractor fraction versus loss). Qi Lu's own defense uses density-based spatial clustering of applications with noise (DBSCAN) on pheromone trails, plus a cluster grouping method and adaptive timing [S-LQ-21]. A new, simpler rule compared against a reimplementation would be the contribution (inference).
- Venues (inference): Towards Autonomous Robotic Systems (TAROS, where Qi Lu published this line [S-LQ-2]), ANTS (International Conference on Swarm Intelligence), International Conference on Ubiquitous Robots (UR).

### Related papers
- Luna and Qi Lu, robust mitigation of misleading pheromone trails, TAROS 2024 proceedings, 2025. Reports foraging decline as the detractor share rises, and tests the defense with obstacles [S-LQ-21].
- Qi Lu pheromone-trail papers, UR 2024 and ICCCR 2024 labels with links [S-LQ-2]. Titles not read (partial).
- Qi Lu, dynamic robot chains with ARGoS, ICRA 2021 and 2022 [S-LQ-2].

## Project: Search-pattern foraging: spiral vs random walk vs hybrid

Rank 2. Score R2 F3 Fit3 = 18.
Course topics: motion planning, swarm robotics, search agents.

### Problem
- Deterministic spiral search finds near food first but scales poorly. Random-walk search scales but wastes time. Partial: from a search snippet of the abstract [S-LQ-5].

### Goal
- Rebuild a multi-region bidirectional spiral search and compare it with random-walk search as swarm size grows.

### How it works in ARGoS
- Robots: 4 to 32 foot-bots, one nest in the center.
- Sensors: positioning (for spiral waypoints), ground sensor (food spots), proximity.
- Behaviour rules: spiral waypoint following per region. Two robots in one region start at opposite spiral ends. Partial: snippet [S-LQ-5].
- Loop functions: food placement (uniform, clustered, power-law), floor spots, count of food delivered [S-LQ-9].

### Algorithm sketch
1. Split the arena into k equal square regions.
2. Assign each robot to an unclaimed region or a region with one robot. Partial: snippet [S-LQ-5].
3. Robot 1 walks the region spiral outward, robot 2 walks it inward.
4. On food, go to nest, drop, return to the last spiral point.
5. Baseline: correlated random walk with return to nest.

### What to measure
- Food collected in fixed time versus swarm size (4, 8, 16, 32) and food layout.
- Crossover swarm size where random walk beats spiral.

### Research potential and venues
- Open question: when does a deterministic plan beat a stochastic one, as a function of swarm size and food distribution [S-LQ-5].
- New beyond a demo: a hybrid that switches from spiral to random walk on a crowding signal (inference).
- Venues (inference): Conference on Robots and Vision (CRV, where Qi Lu published the spiral paper per Scholar [S-LQ-3]), ANTS, Distributed Autonomous Robotic Systems (DARS).

### Related papers
- Qi Lu, Ryan Luna, multi-region bidirectional spiral search, 2023, ARGoS [S-LQ-5].
- Qi Lu et al., physical vs simulated deterministic and stochastic foraging, ICRA 2019 [S-LQ-2].

## Project: Adaptive resource distribution (precision irrigation)

Rank 4 (tie). Score R3 F2 Fit2 = 12. Swing score: see 2.0.
Course topics: swarm robotics, sensors, multi-robot systems, motion planning (baseline).
Replaces the earlier "throwing" reading. The owner meant placing a resource where the need is.

### Problem
- A field dries unevenly. Some cells need water now, others later.
- Nobody has a map of the need. Robots must sense it.
- Qi Lu's foraging swarms target agriculture among other uses [S-LQ-4]. Delivering water is foraging in reverse: carry from one source to many sinks (inference).

### Goal
- Keep the largest share of the field above a moisture threshold with the least water wasted, using no map and no central planner.

### How it works in ARGoS
- Robots: 10 to 30 foot-bots. One water source zone.
- Sensors: ground sensor (reads dryness as floor gray level), positioning, proximity, range-and-bearing (recruit messages).
- Behaviour rules: search for dry cells. Go to source, fill, return, pour. Broadcast "dry here" to recruit nearby robots.
- Loop functions: hidden moisture grid. Each cell dries at its own rate (uneven). Floor color shows moisture through `GetFloorColor`, repainted by `SetChanged()` [S-LQ-9]. Pouring raises cell moisture. Water poured on a wet cell counts as waste.

### Algorithm sketch
1. Each step, loop functions lower each cell's moisture by its own drying rate.
2. An empty robot random-walks or follows a recruit message toward reported dry spots.
3. On a dry cell it records the position, goes to the source, fills one unit.
4. It returns to the recorded spot and pours on the driest cell in reach.
5. While returning it broadcasts "dry at (x, y)" over range-and-bearing.
6. Robot failure test: disable k random robots at time t.

Baselines:
- Random: robots pour wherever they are when full.
- Centralized planner: the planner sees the true moisture grid and sends each robot to the driest unclaimed cell by A* path (course motion planning).

### Variant: demand-driven delivery
- Supplies appear at a source. Requests pop up at unknown places and times.
- Key difference: in delivery, demand is announced when it appears. In irrigation, demand is hidden and must be sensed.
- Robots decide among themselves who serves which request. No central dispatcher.
- Compare three decentralized methods:
  - Greedy nearest: the closest free robot takes the request (inference).
  - Response threshold: a robot takes a request when its stimulus exceeds its own threshold (inference, no source fetched).
  - Auction with consensus: consensus-based bundle algorithm (CBBA) [S-LQ-38]. Partial: record page rate-limited.
- When a swarm wins: no infrastructure (disaster relief), robot failures, or large scale. Otherwise a central dispatcher wins (inference).

### Variant: drone swarm crop spraying
Owner request (2026-11-10): a user marks an area, and the drones spray it well.

Problem:
- A user marks a field area. Several drones spray it.
- Each cell must get the right dose. No cell is missed. No cell is sprayed twice.
- Key difference from irrigation: the need map is given (a prescription map). Robots do not sense it.
- Tanks and batteries are small. Drones leave to refill, so the team changes during the job.

Why a swarm (inference unless cited):
- Area size: one drone's chemical load forces many landings to reload. That downtime motivates multi-UAV spraying [S-LQ-45].

- Core issue: dynamic re-partitioning. A drone leaves to refill or fails. Its neighbors must take over its cells with local information only, then hand them back on return.
- A central planner is the baseline. It sees the whole map and replans on each refill (inference).

## Project: Risk-aware collective navigation in an unknown hazardous field

Rank 4 (tie). Score R3 F2 Fit2 = 12.
Course topics: motion planning (A*), swarm robotics, multi-robot systems, sensors.
Civilian framing: rescue or inspection robots crossing fire, radiation or chemical zones.

### Problem
- A swarm must cross a field to a goal region. Parts of the field disable robots.
- Nobody knows where the danger is at the start. The swarm learns by losing robots or by sensing.

### Goal
- Reach the goal with the largest surviving fraction, by sharing danger information as robots find it.

### How it works in ARGoS
- Robots: 20 to 40 foot-bots, start region and goal region painted on the floor.
- Sensors: positioning, proximity, range-and-bearing (danger messages), ground sensor (optional hazard hint).
- Variant (a) hazard zones: hidden zones. The loop function disables a robot inside one with probability p per step.
- Variant (b) surveillance: sentries with view cones, static or patrolling. A robot inside a cone is detected and lost (or the mission fails).
- Loop functions: hazard and sentry logic, kill list, a shared risk grid drawn on the floor as a "danger pheromone" [S-LQ-9].

### Algorithm sketch
1. Each robot plans an A* path on a grid. Edge cost = distance + w × risk of the cell.
2. When a robot is disabled, its last cell gets high risk (neighbors heard its last messages).
3. Survivors broadcast risk updates over range-and-bearing. Or the loop function paints them as danger pheromone that decays.
4. Robots replan when their path crosses a cell whose risk rose.
5. Sweep w from 0 (shortest path) to high (most cautious).

Baselines:
- Individual shortest path (w = 0, no sharing).
- Plain flocking toward the goal.
- Swarm with danger sharing.

### What to measure
- Survival fraction. Time to goal.
- Trade-off curve: survival versus time, one point per w value.

### Research potential and venues
- Open question: how much does shared danger information raise survival, and what does it cost in time (inference).
- Related lines: routing with attrition risk [UNVERIFIED] [S-LQ-35], a shared risk map in a swarm [S-LQ-33], collective evasion of an intruder [S-LQ-34], minimal-exposure paths against a patrolling observer [S-LQ-32].
- New beyond a demo: the survival-time trade-off curve for a swarm with stigmergic danger marks (inference).
- Venues (inference): ANTS, DARS, IROS or ICRA safety and resilience workshops.

### Qi Lu related work
- No paper on hazard or sentry navigation found on his pages [S-LQ-2], [S-LQ-3].
- Nearest link: his pheromone-security work. Detractors lay misleading trails that lead to forager capture and removal, so robot loss is already a measure in his paper [S-LQ-21]. A danger pheromone is the protective twin of that idea (inference).

### Related papers

- Novák et al., fast collective evasion in UAV swarms, Bioinspiration and Biomimetics 2021 (arXiv 2024) [S-LQ-34].

## Project: Collective sorting and segregation

Rank 7. Score R2 F2 Fit2 = 8.
Course topics: swarm robotics, sensors.

### Problem
- A swarm must split mixed items or robots into groups, each group in its own place, without a leader.
- Ant brood sorting inspired a simple rule: pick up lonely items, drop near similar ones (Deneubourg et al. 1991) [UNVERIFIED].

### Goal
- Sort two item colors into two clusters, or segregate two robot types, and measure cluster purity over time.

### How it works in ARGoS
- Robots: 10 to 20 foot-bots.
- Sensors: ground sensor (item color as floor spot), positioning.
- Behaviour rules: pick-up probability falls with similar items nearby. Drop probability rises with similar items nearby.
- Loop functions: items as colored floor spots, local-density count for each item [S-LQ-9].
- Robot segregation variant: different attraction for same and other type (differential potential) [S-LQ-15].

### Algorithm sketch
1. Scatter red and blue items.
2. Robot random walks. On an item, compute fraction f of same-color items nearby (loop function supplies it).
3. Pick up with probability (k1 / (k1 + f))^2. Formula form attributed to Deneubourg et al. 1991, not read [UNVERIFIED].
4. Carry. Drop where same-color fraction is high, probability (f / (k2 + f))^2 [UNVERIFIED].
5. Variant: add a nest per color (multi-place, like Qi Lu's multiple-place foraging [S-LQ-2]).

### What to measure
- Number of clusters and purity per color over time.
- Time to 90 percent purity versus swarm size.

### Research potential and venues
- Open question: sorting speed and purity with minimal sensing (inference).
- New beyond a demo: sorting into fixed depots chosen by the swarm (inference).
- Venues (inference): ANTS, SWARM (International Symposium on Swarm Behavior and Bio-Inspired Robotics).

### Related papers

### Problem
